# Betelgeuse and its ‘Betel-buddy’ could light up Earth’s skies

*The famous star Betelgeuse may be poised to engulf a companion star, releasing a brilliant eruption a few millennia down the road*

One of the night sky’s most storied stars seems to have an even brighter future ahead of it. Betelgeuse—the 10th-brightest star, as seen from Earth, perched at the shoulder of the Orion constellation—has been studied for millennia by generations of stargazers, some of whom saw it shift from yellow to a red hue. Someday this red supergiant star will probably go out in an astrophysical blaze of glory, exploding as a supernova that will light up Earth’s skies. But a new preprint study suggests the iconic star will perform a surprising penultimate flourish before its demise. In a few thousand years’ time, Betelgeuse should swallow its recently revealed companion star and produce a brilliant eruption, shining even brighter than Venus in the heavens above.

Betelgeuse has already shown its flare for the dramatic in the past few years. In an event called the star’s “Great Dimming” in 2019, Betelgeuse mysteriously dimmed, seeming like it might explode. Instead astronomers found that it had merely been obscured by dust formed from material it ejected. In recent years researchers also found indications that Betelgeuse harbors a hidden companion star, with some of the most tantalizing evidence emerging just this summer.

As ideas about Betelgeuse and its “Betel-buddy” began to emerge, so did new questions about the duo’s ultimate fate, says Naunet Leonhardes-Barboza, lead author of the paper and a graduate student at the University of California, Santa Cruz. The companion’s presence, it seems, complicates Betelgeuse’s long, now not-so-lonely journey into cosmic night. “Does that mean that the end stages of its life are going to look different now? Are these two stars going to merge?” Leonhardes-Barboza says.


## A Sight to Behold


In pursuit of an answer, she and her colleagues modeled how the star system might evolve over time—examining how the companion star could spiral down to its doom, to be torn apart once it is engulfed by Betelgeuse. The team set up simulations for three different possible masses of the companion star—from 0.7 to two times the mass of the Sun—and found that the more massive the companion was, the more energetic and luminous the outburst that was unleashed by its collision would be. All of the modeled masses led to the creation of what astronomers call a luminous red nova. The eruption, which would likely occur within the next 10,000 years, Leonhardes-Barboza says, could be 1.5 to three times brighter than Venus, as it appears from Earth, making Betelgeuse potentially visible even during the daytime. Long after the star’s “Great Dimming” made headlines, it seems that Betelgeuse will bestow a “Great Brightening” on Earthlings, too.

“For future generations, it’ll be incredible to behold,” says Jared Goldberg, an astrophysicist at the Flatiron Institute’s Center for Computational Astrophysics, who was not part of the study. The experience, Leonhardes-Barboza says, might be akin to how 16th-century stargazers marveled at a supernova—famously noticed by astronomer Tycho Brahe—while it shone brightly in the sky for 16 months. “It would be really noticeable,” Leonhardes-Barboza says. “You’d be like, ‘Whoa, what’s that really bright star there?’” Betegeuse’s brightening, however, would last only a couple days.

While the episode will certainly be a treat for future generations, the study’s forecast offers insights for today’s astronomers too, Goldberg says. For instance, while Betelgeuse’s proximity to Earth offers us practically a front-row seat to its eventual celestial fireworks, other, farther-off red supergiants might only be detected when they experience similar boosts in brightness. By clearly linking the predicted type of outburst with the anticipated astrophysical merger of Betelgeuse and its Betel-buddy, astronomers can better calibrate their searches for similar events, Goldberg says. “That gives observers a roadmap on how to point their telescopes and search through the data that we’re constantly collecting,” he adds.

For Leonhardes-Barboza, the lead-up to Betelgeuse’s great brightening will also be a historic event in its own right. This opportunity to consistently study a premerger system is a rare one, she says: “The fact that we have so much data and knowledge of Betelgeuse’s current state and will be able to follow the system up until the stellar merger—I mean, we don’t have that for anything else.”
