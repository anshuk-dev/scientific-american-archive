# Did SpaceX catch its Starship?

*The 15th spaceflight of SpaceX’s giant launch vehicle aimed for something unprecedented: snatching both rocket stages from midair after a journey to orbit. The test proved partially successful but still ended in a historic first*

Starship Flight 15 puts SpaceX’s moonshots to the test

SpaceX’s Starship, the largest and most ambitious rocket ever built, has finished its most consequential test yet with a historic catch on November 14.

Elon Musk says the enormous two-stage vehicle will someday carry humans to the moon and Mars, but more immediately, Starship is central to the company’s plan to build out its Starlink satellite Internet constellation (not to mention its aim to pack a million data center satellites into orbit around Earth). But first SpaceX must show Starship can deliver on its most transformative and critical promise: full reusability.

That’s why this latest test, Flight 15, has been so consequential. The Starship orbiter—called “Ship 42”—lifted off atop its Super Heavy booster at 5:47 P.M. EST on November 13 from the company’s Starbase site in South Texas. This test flight had a deceptively simple goal: getting Ship 42 into orbit and then returning both it and the booster back to Earth intact and essentially ready to fly again.

What that involved was the booster and orbiter separately reentering Earth’s atmosphere and flying back to Starbase, where the launch tower, fitted with enormous mechanical arms, could catch them. SpaceX had previously shown it can pluck the Super Heavy from the air in previous tests. But Flight 15 was the first attempt to catch the orbiter itself.

After spending nearly a full day in space and circling Earth some 14 times, Ship 42 began its homecoming, firing its engines just before 3:30 P.M. EST on November 14 to deorbit and fly back to Starbase.

The fateful moment came with Starship’s final landing burn at 4:20 P.M as it plunged through the atmosphere. Seconds later, the orbiter performed a “belly flop” maneuver, turning end-over-end and swiveling its fiery engine plumes downward to slow and slide precisely between the awaiting giant arms. They swung closed around the midair vehicle, like chopsticks snatching the world’s largest egg roll.

With Starship, SpaceX has now achieved another world first, something no orbital-class rocket has done before: bringing it back home and catching it one piece.

One Flight, Not Quite Two Catches

The mission began with the ignition of the 33 engines on Starship’s Super Heavy booster, which propelled the spacecraft toward orbit before the two vehicles separated. The booster was then expected to return to Starbase about six and a half minutes after liftoff for a precision catch by the tower’s awaiting mechanical arms. But it instead diverted into an offshore splashdown after trouble with some of its engines.

“Slight underperformance of engines on this flight is the reason” for Super Heavy’s diversion, Musk said in an X post a few hours before the orbiter’s planned return.

Meanwhile Starship itself continued into orbit and successfully deployed 26 next-generation satellites for Starlink, SpaceX’s fast-growing Internet business. Once it’s fully operational, Starship will be able to loft Starlink satellites in far larger and more frequent batches than the firm’s retiring workhorse rocket, the Falcon 9. That will pave the way for the company’s plans to supplement Starlink with the launch of huge numbers of orbital data centers for artificial intelligence.

Even if Flight 15 had seen a flawless recovery of both stages, the real prize remains untested: SpaceX must still show that it can rapidly turn around both the booster and orbiter for a fast return to flight. That would put the company well on its way to fulfilling Musk’s projection of roughly one Starship launch per week in the first half of 2027.

Flight 15 follows Flight 14, which saw Starship successfully reach orbit and deploy a batch of operational Starlink satellites. But that previous mission ended early because of an engine issue on Starship, leading SpaceX to bring the vehicle down in the Pacific Ocean. (The Super Heavy booster splashed down as planned in the Gulf of Mexico.) Besides demonstrating a much more audacious recovery, Flight 15 was also Starship’s longest-yet sojourn in orbit.

A Moonshot in Waiting

The test is essential prep for Starship to meet its obligations to NASA’s Artemis program, which seeks to return U.S. astronauts to the moon before the end of 2028. SpaceX is developing a modified Human Landing System (HLS) version of Starship to potentially serve as a lunar lander, transporting crews between lunar orbit and the moon’s surface.

Before it can ferry humans on those far-flung voyages, though, Starship has to demonstrate key capabilities closer to home. During NASA’s Artemis III mission, targeted for next summer, Starship is set to launch to rendezvous and dock in low-Earth orbit with a NASA Orion spacecraft carrying four astronauts; SpaceX’s rival Blue Origin is also part of that mission, and a test version of that company’s Blue Moon Mark 2 lander will also link up with Orion.

Future Starship test flights will also need to solve the challenge of in-space refueling because, for the heavy HLS, a round trip to the moon requires more fuel than a single Starship can carry into orbit.

SpaceX’s ambitions are unfolding against a changing political landscape as well, particularly in Texas, where the company’s Starbase facility is based. The state’s governor-elect Gina Hinojosa campaigned against energy-intensive data centers and has pledged to pause new approvals until stronger protections are in place.

The timing is awkward for SpaceX: the company has just disclosed plans for a proposed $24-billion gas-fired power plant and supercomputing complex at Starbase and is seeking local tax incentives for the project.
