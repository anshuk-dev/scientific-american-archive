# Math students fear AI will take their jobs—they’re using it anyway

*A survey of 268 graduate students and postdocs reveals researchers are unsure of their future in mathematics and uneasy about using AI—the very technology they fear*

**By Sanidhya Sharma** | Edited by Joseph Howlett &bull; **2026-11-16** &bull; 5 min read

Everyone has heard what the giants of mathematics think about artificial intelligence—the open letters, the op-eds, the predictions from laureates who have already cemented their reputation. Almost no one has asked the people whose future is less secure—the graduate students and postdoctoral researchers left to pick up the pieces after AI continues to plough through the field of math.

To take the field’s pulse, three math Ph.D. students—Anna Brandenberger, Hanna Mularczyk and Dora Woodruff—launched an online Google Forms survey in the wake of AI cracking problems that mathematicians had chased for decades. The survey quickly spread through word of mouth and department mailing lists: “We needed something that features the voices of grad students and postdocs as well,” Woodruff says.

Described in a preprint paper, the 268 responses from nearly 60 institutions paint a stark picture of a community in limbo: largely frustrated and anxious that AI is taking this field for a ride yet divided on what to do about it.

*Sanidhya Sharma; Source: “Early Career Researchers’ Responses to AI in Math,” by Anna Brandenberger, Hanna Mularczyk and Dora Woodruff. Version 2. Preprint posted to arXiv.org on November 4, 2026*


Seven in 10 respondents say they’re now less likely to remain in math, with broad consensus that AI will make tenure-track jobs harder to get. Two-thirds report feeling less motivated to work on their research, with the growing possibility of a large language model (LLM) beating them to their goals.

“People have said things to me like, ‘I feel useless,’” Woodruff says. “It’s not just people’s jobs—math is a lot of people’s identities.” Some, she says, feel that “not only does their work not matter, but their minds and their creativity don’t matter.”

While senior mathematicians debate what the field might look like in 20 years, her and her colleagues’ respondents are worried about the immediate future. Woodruff sums up what many of her early-career colleagues are thinking: “In one year or two years,” as she puts it, “I don’t know if I’m going to have a job or funding,” she says.

But in spite of this universal malaise, young mathematicians are split roughly down the middle on the technology itself: about half are optimistic about AI’s potential for mathematics, while a third oppose its use—either outright or for creative mathematical work. The optimists come with an asterisk, though: among them, four out of five say they’re excited about AI in theory but strongly opposed to how things are unfolding in practice. Just 13 percent of all respondents are happy with the current trajectory.

Even among the respondents who do use AI—which is nearly all of them—not many seem to actually enjoy it. In written responses, many describe feeling pressured to use AI to keep pace, resulting in a generation of mathematicians who have been reluctantly mastering the very technology they fear could make them obsolete.

Woodruff cautions that the voluntary survey isn’t representative of all young mathematicians, and nearly all of its responses arrived before OpenAI published AI-assisted solutions to hundreds of open problems in October. But it shows that this headline-making year for math has come at a huge cost.

“A lot of people outside of math are looking at what’s happening in math now,” Woodruff says. “So far, we’re not doing a particularly inspiring job.”
