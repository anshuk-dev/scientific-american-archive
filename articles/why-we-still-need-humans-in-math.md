# Why we still need humans in math

*Mathematicians are calling it a “crisis.” Our editor thinks the field will survive but only if it changes*

**By Jeanna Bryner** | Edited by Jeanna Bryner &bull; **2026-11-17** &bull; 5 min read

My twin sister studied to be a chemical engineer, which required quite a bit of math. She once told me that she often finished exams later than her classmates, not because she was getting stuck but because she enjoyed deriving thermodynamic equations. She could have memorized the formulas and moved on. Instead she rebuilt them from first principles, line by line, because seeing where they came from was the fun part.

That kind of joy is suddenly at risk. Mathematics may be the first field where artificial intelligence isn’t just assisting experts but doing the work that makes them experts. Headlines have been awash with such feats, including the September solution of the Navier-Stokes equations—a problem that had stumped mathematicians for nearly 200 years—by one of OpenAI’s unreleased internal models. In our cover story, senior reporter Joseph Howlett asks whether math as we know it is about to end. The mathematicians he spoke with are worried, using words such as “crisis” and “collapse” to describe AI’s intrusion into their field. My view is that math will survive but only by leaning harder into humans’ unique contributions to it.

After all, “math as we know it” has been a moving target. In ancient Mesopotamia “math” meant surveying fields and tracking stars. To Plato and Euclid, it was a path to eternal truth. And for Galileo and Newton, it was the language of nature. Today it’s the backbone of the very technology that is now unsettling mathematicians.

Along the way, the field has overcome or adapted to one disruptive tool after another. Fibonacci’s written place-value arithmetic eventually put abacus makers out of business. In 1976 mathematicians proved the four-color theorem with a computer that checked nearly 2,000 configurations, far more than any person could verify by hand. The field argued earnestly over whether a proof no human could check counted as a proof at all. More recently my parent friends have been concerned that kids who use fancy calculators won’t learn how calculations are done.

Those tools, however, automated parts of the process that people knew how to do, even if they did them slowly. AI is different. Artificially intelligent models can work things out from start to finish: in the Navier-Stokes achievement, an agent swarm of about 10,000 copies of the unreleased frontier model was divvied up into groups to attack the problem from a range of perspectives, with another computer system flagging promising findings and deploying still other groups of agents to follow up. Mathematicians don’t know whether the resulting proof is valid or what reasoning the AI used to get there.

This achievement, and many others on its heels, has mathematicians questioning their raison d’être.

Yet look closer: Without humans, AI chatbots would be empty shells. Who builds these models? Who decides which problems they’ll wrestle with? Who interprets and appreciates their results? Humans are involved at every step. Rather than becoming a machine-only discipline, math is likely to pivot and move yet again toward what we humans contribute most: our judgment about which questions matter, which equations are beautiful and how we turn a proof into understanding.

One researcher told Howlett “mathematicians may be the canary in the coal mine for a lot of other professions.” If so, people in fields such as journalism and filmmaking, to name a couple, will soon face the same questions: Why do we do what we do, and how can we keep finding meaning in it?

In a quote attributed to Euclid, the mathematician told King Ptolemy I of Egypt, “There is no royal road to geometry.” AI is seemingly a shortcut, but mathematicians get to decide whether it’s worth it. The joy they get from the journey is the most human part of math—and the part most worth protecting.
